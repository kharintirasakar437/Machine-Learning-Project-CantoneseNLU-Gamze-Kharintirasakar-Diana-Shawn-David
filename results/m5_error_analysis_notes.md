# Error Analysis

The tuned logistic-regression model produced 233 validation errors on the sentiment task. Of these, 221 involve either a true or predicted `ok` label, showing that the main source of confusion remains the boundary between the neutral class and the positive or negative classes. Several sampled reviews contain mixed evidence, such as strong praise for some dishes alongside criticism of price, service, or other menu items. These examples suggest that character n-gram features can capture local positive and negative expressions but may have difficulty determining the overall sentiment of a longer review when conflicting cues are present. However, not all errors are borderline: some clearly negative reviews were predicted as positive, and some strongly positive reviews were predicted as negative.

For language detection, the tuned logistic-regression model produced 501 validation errors. Of these, 496 involve the code-mixed class. Specifically, 125 Mandarin examples and 95 Cantonese examples were predicted as code-mixed, while 150 code-mixed examples were predicted as Mandarin and 126 as Cantonese. Direct Cantonese-to-Mandarin and Mandarin-to-Cantonese confusion occurred only five times in total. This indicates that the model generally distinguishes the two pure-language classes well, while the main difficulty lies in identifying the boundary between pure and code-mixed text. The sampled code-mixed examples also show that some class-2 sentences contain only subtle or synthetic deviations from an otherwise pure Cantonese or Mandarin sentence, making the boundary inherently difficult to model.

# Draft Conclusions

The final Milestone 5 validation results show that tuned character-level TF-IDF with L2 logistic regression is the strongest model for both Cantonese sentiment analysis and language detection. The best configuration for both tasks uses `C=30` with character 1–2 grams. It reaches a validation macro-F1 of 0.769 on sentiment analysis and 0.744 on language detection.

For sentiment analysis, the main remaining difficulty is the neutral `ok` class, which is involved in 221 of the model's 233 validation errors. Reviews containing both positive and negative comments are especially difficult, although some strongly positive and strongly negative examples are also misclassified.

For language detection, nearly all remaining errors involve the code-mixed class. Of 501 validation errors, 496 involve class 2, while direct confusion between pure Cantonese and Mandarin occurs only five times. This suggests that the model has learned useful distinctions between the two pure-language classes but has greater difficulty identifying when a sentence should be considered code-mixed.

Overall, the results show that relatively simple character-based classical models can form strong baselines for Cantonese NLU. At the same time, the error analysis shows that overall sentiment balance and subtle mixed-language boundaries remain important challenges.

# Draft Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the current results. First, all Milestone 5 model selection and error analysis are based only on the validation split. The test split remains locked and will only be used for the final evaluation, so the current results should not be interpreted as final estimates of generalization performance.

Second, the sentiment dataset is based on restaurant reviews from OpenRice. This provides realistic Cantonese text, but limits the domain of the conclusions. Performance on restaurant reviews may not transfer directly to other forms of Cantonese sentiment, such as social media, news comments, or conversational text. Many reviews are also long and contain both positive and negative opinions about different aspects of a restaurant. A single document-level label may therefore hide substantial within-review variation.

Third, the language-detection task includes a synthetic code-mixed class. The error analysis shows that this class accounts for most of the remaining confusion. Some code-mixed examples contain only subtle changes from an otherwise pure Cantonese or Mandarin sentence, while other examples appear linguistically unusual. The project has also identified known label conflicts in the upstream language-detection data. As a result, some apparent model errors may reflect ambiguous, synthetic, or noisy examples rather than model failure alone.

Finally, the classical models evaluated in this project rely heavily on character-level statistical patterns. These features are computationally efficient and perform strongly as baselines, but they do not explicitly model longer-range meaning, discourse context, or the relationship between multiple opinions within a review. The frozen LaBSE plus kNN approach adds pretrained multilingual semantic representations, but its validation performance remains below the tuned TF-IDF logistic-regression model on both tasks.
